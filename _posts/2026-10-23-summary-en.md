After obtaining administrative control, they reconfigured 333 Tor exit relays to redirect port-22 SSH connections to 12 attacker-controlled interception servers; users who ignored warnings that the host key had changed could expose their passwords. The relays continued forwarding encrypted web traffic normally but blocked ports 25, 465, 587, 993, and 995, which are associated with encrypted email, while allowing unencrypted email traffic in an attempt to capture plaintext messages. rss · Ars Technica · Oct 22, 11:30

**「Background」** Tor exit relays form the final hop of a Tor circuit, so they can observe traffic that lacks end-to-end encryption, while HTTPS remains protected between the user and the destination. SSH host keys help clients detect a changed destination, and email security depends on the protocol and whether the service permits plaintext fallback or downgrade.

**「Impact」** Users whose SSH connections traversed an affected relay faced a risk of credential theft if they accepted the changed host key; an accompanying Telegram report says the relays were marked BadExit on October 4 and taken offline on October 5.

### [ThinkingBox Turns Database State Checks into RL Rewards](https://huggingface.co/blog/microsoft/thinkingbox-training) ⭐️ 7.0/10

Microsoft and Hugging Face released the open-source ThinkingBox-Training project, which converts executable checks of an agent’s final database state into reinforcement-learning rewards for tasks outside ThinkingBox-Bench. The benchmark’s 507 tasks launch real backends, expose agents to MCP tools and a simulated user, and grade terminal database records rather than requiring a particular tool sequence. To create a fixed training pool, the team estimated each task’s success probability once under the base policy—a method called offline policy-relative difficulty—and then trained Qwen3.8-27B for 50 GRPO steps. On the sealed, out-of-distribution benchmark, average success rose from 51.70% to 60.78%, while tasks completed correctly in all 20 attempts increased from 38 to 107, or 7.50% to 21.10%. The improvement in repeatability came with a small reduction in pass@20 from 453 to 446 tasks.

rss · Hugging Face Blog · Oct 22, 22:24

**「Background」** ThinkingBox-Bench evaluates tool-using agents by starting a backend, allowing them to act through MCP tools, and then checking the terminal database state rather than prescribing a particular sequence of calls. ThinkingBox-Training applies those executable outcome checks as reinforcement-learning rewards: it selects tasks according to their difficulty for the current policy, then uses GRPO to post-train an agent and assess whether improvements transfer to held-out tasks.

**「Impact」** The release gives developers an outcome-based way to train database agents while measuring all-attempt consistency alongside average success, a combination that better reflects reliability when agents modify production records.

### [Global Forest Typology map resolves land use at 10 meters](https://deepmind.google/blog/beyond-tree-cover-mapping-the-worlds-forest-types/) ⭐️ 7.0/10

Google DeepMind and Google Research, with WRI and IIASA, released Forest Typology \(ForTy\) 2020, a global 10-meter-resolution map that distinguishes primary forests, naturally regenerating forests, planted forests, plantations, tree crops or agroforestry, and other land. An early-release annual time series covers 2017–2025 and tracks not only forest loss and regrowth but also changes such as natural forest becoming a timber plantation. ### [Day-0 vLLM Support for MiniMax-M3.1-Flash](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-10-23-minimax-m31-flash) ⭐️ 8.0/10

rss · vLLM Blog · Oct 23, 00:00

**「Background」** MiniMax-M3.1-Flash targets efficient inference, but its block-sparse attention and quantization-aware NVFP4 caches make serving hardware-dependent: each backend must cut memory traffic without changing block selection or paying heavy conversion costs. The post covers vLLM’s day-0 support across NVIDIA Blackwell, Rubin, and AMD MI350.

**「Solution」** MSA splits KV into 128-token blocks; an indexer scans and max-pools scores, then each GQA group retains its top 16 blocks, with the latest always kept, capping attention at 2,048 keys. According to the author, long-context decode HBM traffic falls to roughly 12.5% of dense attention despite the full index scan. On NVIDIA, vLLM fuses projection, normalization, RoPE, quantization, and cache insertion; parallelizes exact indexer selection; tunes NVFP4 conversion and MegaMoE; and uses FlashInfer PrimiTS for the DSpark drafter. Its indexer kernels dequantize NVFP4 to FP8 on Blackwell but FP16 on Rubin; reported gains include up to 3.3× faster indexing at 512K on 4×GB300 and about 19% lower P50 TTFT for 512-plus-token TP4 prefill. The AMD path adds lookup-based dequantization, context and sequence parallelism, dual-batch overlap, contiguous KV transfer, and a one-launch sparse-MoE decode kernel, reportedly speeding 16K-plus prefill by 1.8–2.1× on 4×MI355X and TP4 low-concurrency decode by up to 2.3× on MI350X. At the latency-sensitive end of vendor-run AgentX results, throughput reaches 418.9 tokens/s per user on GB300 and 724.9 on Rubin; AMD’s headline frontier emphasizes tokens per dollar, but the limited methodology cautions against broad cross-platform comparisons.

**「Takeaway」** The article’s thesis is that MiniMax-M3.1-Flash’s serving gains emerge from coordinating algorithmic sparsity, low-precision formats, fused kernels, and parallel execution rather than from any single optimization.
