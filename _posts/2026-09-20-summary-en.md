### [The shipping gap: why gifted builders must push past taste](https://seangoedecke.com/grit-your-teeth-and-ship-it/) ⭐️ 4.0/10

rss · Sean Goedecke · Sep 20, 00:00

**「Background」** Sean Goedecke opens with Ira Glass&\#x27;s familiar &\#x27;gap&\#x27; quote: creative people enter their fields because of good taste, but for the first couple of years their work doesn&\#x27;t actually match that taste. That mismatch between aspiration and output is what makes shipping painful.

**「Solution」** Goedecke argues the tension is acute for gifted programmers, whose preference for elegant, correct systems makes them emotionally bothered by flaws and reluctant to ship. In large codebases — where consistency matters more than local correctness — the right move is sometimes to duplicate existing flaws rather than refactor them. Without that flexibility, gifted programmers freeze up, retreat to safe chores like polishing dev environments or rewriting tests, or spiral into shame and quit. He illustrates the same pattern in his own writing: his sensitive ear for awkward prose makes drafting unpleasant, but he publishes anyway because shipping is a skill that improves with repetition, and when he publishes daily each draft feels better. Looking back, he finds no correlation between his confidence in a draft and its popularity — some posts he thought were throwaway resonated strongly, while carefully crafted ones didn&\#x27;t. The lesson, he says, is that you can&\#x27;t predict what audiences will value, so you must be momentum-based rather than outcome-based: produce at high volume and see what sticks, even revisiting the same idea repeatedly until you get it right. He notes he&\#x27;s written roughly thirty posts on shipping and related topics, and expects to keep writing them as long as the ideas interest him.

**「Takeaway」** Goedecke&\#x27;s core point: building and shipping are separate skills, and gifted creators must grit their teeth and ship past their own dissatisfaction — because in both codebases and creative work, consistency and momentum reliably outperform individually polished pieces.
