### [Tesla launches high-volume Semi production, but megawatt-charging network lags](https://arstechnica.com/cars/2026/09/teslas-big-electric-truck-faces-an-even-bigger-infrastructure-challenge/) ⭐️ 6.0/10

Tesla officially began high-volume production of its Semi electric Class 8 truck at a livestreamed, invite-only event at its Sparks, Nevada factory, nearly a decade after first unveiling the concept in late 2017. Tesla VP of Vehicle Engineering Lars Moravy confirmed at the event that the long-range version will travel an estimated 500 miles per charge—roughly the average daily mileage of American long-haul truckers—while the standard version is rated for about 325 miles, both above the 155–430-mile range advertised by competing electric semi makers. The bigger hurdle is charging infrastructure: the trucks require specialized megawatt-class chargers to refill their large batteries in reasonable time, and Tesla&\#x27;s website currently lists only two such public chargers operating in the Los Angeles area, with Semi program lead Dan Priestley saying the company plans to open 30 by the end of the year. Electric semis still cost two to three times more upfront than their diesel counterparts, and while electrics account for nearly 30 percent of heavy-duty truck sales in China, US adoption has been slow. The event itself was unusually low-key for Tesla, with CEO Elon Musk absent \(attending a White House China State Dinner with President Xi Jinping\) and a small group of mostly male fleet operators trooping across the stage to a pulsing techno beat.

rss · Ars Technica · Sep 26, 10:45

**「Background」** Tesla unveiled the Semi concept in November 2017 as its entry into the heavy-duty \(Class 8\) electric truck market, targeting long-haul fleet operators with promises of long range and low operating costs. High-volume production was repeatedly delayed in the years that followed, while the company increasingly repositioned itself around robotics and autonomous driving and Musk publicly targeted a $20 trillion valuation on that premise. The infrastructure bottleneck is specific: unlike passenger EVs, Class 8 electric trucks require megawatt-class chargers capable of refilling very large battery packs within a few hours, a network that barely exists outside dedicated depots.

**「Impact」** US fleet operators interested in the Semi will, for now, face a near-empty public megawatt-charging network—two public sites in Los Angeles growing to a planned 30 by year&\#x27;s end—meaning depot-based or private charging infrastructure is effectively required to operate the trucks at scale, on top of a two-to-three-times higher upfront price than diesel Class 8 trucks.

The benchmark 10-year U.S. Treasury yield climbed to 5.23% on Friday, its highest level since 2007, rattling investors. While sticky inflation and growing expectations of further Federal Reserve tightening played a role—CME FedWatch shows a 64% probability of an October rate hike, and University of Michigan year-ahead inflation expectations jumped to 4.6% in September—Macquarie strategist Thierry Wizman argued that heavy bond issuance is the more important driver this year. The federal government is borrowing to finance a large deficit at the same time that AI-related corporate spending is flooding the market with new debt: Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, and Oracle alone issued roughly $132 billion through July, compared with a $35 billion annual average from 2020 to 2024, with broader AI-related issuance potentially reaching $300 billion to $570 billion for the year. Wizman warned that elevated capital-spending plans from hyperscalers and their suppliers are likely to keep bond supply heavy into next year, meaning yields could push higher still and weigh on stocks by raising borrowing costs.
